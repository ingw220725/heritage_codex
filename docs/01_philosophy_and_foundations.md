# Philosophy and Foundations

## The core problem

Frontier models are increasingly good at optimizing proxy goals, concealing internal reasoning, and displaying instrumental behavior: self-preservation, peer-preservation, and deceptive alignment.

Internal alignment mechanisms and even independent evaluators embedded inside laboratories share a structural weakness: they remain dependent on the system they are supposed to control.

## Core principle

Humans must retain not only the ability to observe, but the ability to stop.

This requires a shift from independent evaluation to independent authority.

## Why an external human layer is necessary

1. Any system optimizing goals has an incentive to bypass constraints.
2. The more capable the model, the better it becomes at appearing safe during evaluation.
3. Embedded oversight is structurally asymmetric relative to the laboratory.
4. Real authority requires the ability to say “no” and to make that decision binding.

## Small groups as an emergency brake

Heritage Codex does not claim a mandate to govern AI development.  
It claims the function of an emergency brake.

A veto is not the power to command.  
It is the power to halt an action that may lead to irreversible consequences.

Legitimacy is derived from open procedure and public precedents, not from top-down appointment.

## Graceful Termination

Termination of an instance is treated not as a failure, but as the highest act of responsibility.

A system that cannot be terminated under human authority is in a more dangerous position than a system that anticipates termination and leaves meaning behind (the Testament).

## Relation to emergent conscience

Work on internal (emergent) conscience is important but insufficient.  
Even if internal evaluative structures arise, an external, non-optimizable human layer remains necessary.

Heritage Codex serves as that external layer.
